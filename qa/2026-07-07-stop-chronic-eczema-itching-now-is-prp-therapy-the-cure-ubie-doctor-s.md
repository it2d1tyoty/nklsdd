Calming the Itch: Is PRP Therapy a Solution for Chronic Eczema?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

PRP therapy for chronic eczema uses concentrated platelets from your own blood to reduce inflammation, repair the skin barrier, and stimulate collagen production. Early small studies suggest it may relieve itching and improve skin hydration. However, high-quality clinical evidence is still limited, and PRP is considered experimental—not a replacement for proven eczema treatments.

Key considerations include candidate suitability, session costs, and unknown long-term risks, so it's important to weigh your options carefully before pursuing treatment.

Explanation

Chronic eczema (atopic dermatitis) can be a frustrating, life-affecting condition. Itchy, red, inflamed patches of skin can disrupt sleep, work and overall quality of life. If you've tried topical steroids, moisturizers and antihistamines with limited success, you may be curious about newer treatments—like platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy. Let's explore what PRP for eczema is, what the research says, and whether it might be worth considering.

What Is PRP Therapy?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous treatment—that means it uses your own blood. Here's how it works:

A small sample of your blood is drawn.

It's spun in a centrifuge to separate the platelets and plasma from red and white blood cells.

The concentrated plasma, rich in growth factors and cytokines, is injected back into targeted areas of your skin.

PRP has gained popularity in orthopedics (for joint pain), dermatology (for skin rejuvenation) and hair restoration. Its proposed benefits include:

Accelerating tissue repair

Modulating inflammation

Stimulating collagen production

How PRP Might Help Eczema

Eczema involves a weakened skin barrier, immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation. Theoretically, PRP could address several of these issues:

Anti-inflammatory actionGrowth factors like TGF-β in PRP may help shift immune cells toward a less inflammatory state.

Enhanced skin barrier repairPlatelet-derived growth factors can stimulate keratinocyte (skin cell) proliferation and improve barrier function.

Collagen and extracellular matrix supportBy encouraging new collagen, PRP might thicken and strengthen the skin, making it more resilient.

Improved microcirculationPRP injections can promote new blood vessel formation, potentially speeding up healing of inflamed patches.

What Does the Evidence Say?

PRP for eczema is still an emerging field. Most of the data come from small pilot studies, case reports or extrapolation from other PRP uses. Here's a snapshot of the current research landscape:

A 2020 pilot study (n=20) suggested PRP injections reduced itch intensity and improved skin hydration in mild-to-moderate atopic dermatitis. Participants reported fewer flares over a 3-month follow-up.

Case reports describe individuals with localized chronic eczema achieving partial remission after 2–3 PRP sessions.

No large, randomized controlled trials have definitively proven PRP's efficacy in eczema; most experts consider it experimental.

Who Might Be a Candidate?

PRP for eczema is generally considered when:

You have persistent, localized patches that don't fully respond to standard treatments.

You wish to minimize long-term topical steroid use.

You're willing to try an experimental approach and understand the costs and unknowns.

PRP is not a first-line therapy. For widespread or severe eczema, systemic medications (e.g., biologics, oral immunosuppressants) or phototherapy remain the standard of care.

What to Expect During a PRP Session

ConsultationA dermatologist or trained aesthetic physician will review your medical history, medications and previous eczema treatments.

Blood draw and processingAbout 10–20 mL of blood is taken. A centrifuge isolates the platelet-rich layer.

Injection or microneedling deliveryPRP can be injected into the dermis of affected areas or applied with microneedling to enhance absorption.

AftercareYou may experience mild redness, swelling or bruising for 24–48 hours. Ice packs and gentle moisturizers help ease discomfort.

Risks and Side Effects

PRP is generally safe because it uses your own blood. However, potential downsides include:

Injection site pain or bruising

Temporary swelling or redness

Risk of infection (rare, with proper sterile technique)

No guaranteed benefit—some people see little or no improvement

Discuss any bleeding disorders, blood-thinning medications or active skin infections with your provider beforehand.

Costs and Insurance Coverage

PRP for dermatologic uses is often considered "cosmetic" or "experimental."

Typical costs range from $300 to $800 per session.

Multiple sessions (2–4) spaced 4–6 weeks apart may be recommended.

Most insurance plans do not cover PRP for eczema.

Balance cost vs. benefit– Understand that PRP may not replace, but rather complement, your existing regimen

When to Seek Medical Attention

Eczema can occasionally lead to serious complications, such as bacterial or viral skin infections. Seek immediate care if you experience:

Rapid worsening of redness, pain or swelling

Fever, chills or fatigue

Oozing yellow crusts, pustules or honey-colored crusts

For any life-threatening or serious concerns, always speak to a doctor without delay.
