Explanation

Male hypogonadism occurs when the testes don't produce enough testosterone, the hormone that drives many male characteristics and bodily functions. Testosterone levels naturally decline with age, but true hypogonadism causes more pronounced symptoms and may require treatment. Distinguishing between low testosterone due to male hypogonadism and normal aging can be challenging. Below, we explain how doctors make this diagnosis and what you can do if you're concerned.

What Is Male Hypogonadism?

Male hypogonadism refers to deficient testosterone production or impaired testicular function. It can be:

Primary (testicular): a problem within the testes themselves

Secondary (central): an issue in the hypothalamus or pituitary gland

Mixed: features of both primary and secondary

Left untreated, hypogonadism can affect bone density, muscle mass, mood, sexual function and overall quality of life.

Normal Testosterone Decline vs. Hypogonadism

It's normal for men to lose about 1% of testosterone per year after age 30–40. In most cases, this gradual decline doesn't cause significant symptoms. By contrast, male hypogonadism typically brings:

• More severe fatigue and low energy• Noticeable decrease in libido or erections• Mood changes, such as increased irritability or mild depression• Reduced muscle mass and strength• Thinning bones (osteopenia or osteoporosis)

Step 2: Physical Examination

A physical exam helps identify signs of low testosterone:

Testicular size and consistency

Body fat distribution and muscle mass

Body hair patterns (chest, armpit, pubic hair)

Breast tissue growth (gynecomastia)

Signs of other hormonal issues (e.g., thyroid enlargement)

Any abnormalities—like small or soft testes—raise suspicion for male hypogonadism and guide further testing.

Step 3: Laboratory Testing

Blood tests are essential. Key labs include:

Total Testosterone

Measured in the morning (before 10 a.m.) when levels peak

Two separate tests are often needed to confirm low levels

Free Testosterone or Bioavailable Testosterone

Accounts for hormone not bound to sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG)

Useful if SHBG levels are abnormal (e.g., in obesity, liver disease)

Luteinizing Hormone (LH) and Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)

High LH/FSH with low testosterone suggests primary testicular failure

Low or normal LH/FSH with low testosterone suggests secondary (central) hypogonadism

Prolactin

Elevated levels can point to a pituitary tumor or other central causes

Additional Tests as Needed

Iron studies (hemochromatosis)

Pituitary imaging (MRI) if central hypogonadism is suspected

Genetic testing for Klinefelter syndrome or other inherited conditions

Interpreting the Results

Total testosterone below 300 ng/dL (or local lab reference) on two mornings usually confirms low testosterone.

If LH and FSH are elevated, the problem lies in the testes (primary hypogonadism).

If LH and FSH are low or normal, the issue is likely in the brain's control centers (secondary hypogonadism).

Normal-age decline generally shows only mild testosterone reduction without significant LH/FSH changes or clinical signs of deficiency.

When to Refer to a Specialist

You may need an endocrinologist or urologist if:

Lab results are inconclusive

You have complex hormonal imbalances

Imaging reveals a pituitary lesion

Standard treatment fails or causes side effects

Specialists can dig deeper into rare causes, such as genetic syndromes or chronic systemic diseases.

Treatment Options

Treatment depends on cause, age, fertility desires and overall health:

• Testosterone Replacement Therapy (TRT) – Gels, patches, injections or pellets – Improves energy, mood, sexual function, muscle mass – Requires regular monitoring of testosterone levels, hematocrit, prostate health

• Address Underlying Causes – Treat obesity, diabetes or sleep apnea – Review medications that may lower testosterone – Correct nutritional deficiencies or chronic illnesses

• Fertility Considerations – TRT can suppress sperm production – Alternatives like clomiphene citrate or human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) may be used

Risks and Monitoring

TRT can be life-changing but isn't without risks:

Polycythemia (high red blood cell count)

Acne or oily skin

Breast enlargement or tenderness

Sleep apnea worsening

Potential impacts on prostate health

Regular follow-up visits should include:

Testosterone level checks every 3–6 months

Hematocrit and hemoglobin monitoring

PSA testing and digital rectal exam for prostate health

Assessment of symptom improvement and side effects

Differentiating Normal Aging

If you have mild symptoms and borderline test results, your doctor might attribute changes to normal aging. In these cases:

Lifestyle changes often help: exercise, balanced diet, stress management

Treat other conditions that mimic hypogonadism (depression, thyroid disease)

Re-test testosterone levels after 3–6 months

When to Seek Immediate Medical Advice

While many hypogonadism symptoms develop gradually, some signs warrant prompt attention:

Severe, persistent fatigue interfering with daily life

Sudden breast enlargement or nipple discharge

Signs of pituitary mass (headaches, vision changes)

Mood swings leading to self-harm thoughts

Speak to a healthcare professional or go to an emergency department for any serious or life-threatening concerns.

Next Steps

If you suspect you have male hypogonadism or just want to learn more, start by talking to your primary care doctor. Remember, only a qualified healthcare provider can diagnose and manage hormone disorders. Early assessment and treatment can improve symptoms, quality of life and long-term health. If you experience any serious or sudden health changes, please speak to a doctor or visit the nearest emergency department.
