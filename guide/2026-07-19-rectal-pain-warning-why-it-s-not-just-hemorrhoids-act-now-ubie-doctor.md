Rectal pain affects thousands of people every year. When a twinge or burn strikes "down there," it's tempting to chalk it up to hemorrhoids—especially since they're common and often harmless. But not all rectal pain is due to hemorrhoids. Dismissing discomfort as "just hemorrhoids" can delay the diagnosis of more serious conditions. Below, we explain why this misreading happens, review possible causes of rectal pain, and guide you on when to seek medical help.

Why We Blame Hemorrhoids First

Familiarity & Stigma

Hemorrhoids are widely known. Anecdotes from friends or ads for creams reinforce the idea that rectal pain equals hemorrhoids.

Embarrassment around discussing bowel or rectal issues often leads people to self-diagnose rather than consult a professional.

Overlapping Symptoms

Both hemorrhoids and other anorectal conditions can cause itching, burning, bleeding, or discomfort on the toilet.

Mild pain and spotting of blood often feel the same, whether from hemorrhoids, an anal fissure, or early inflammation.

Ease of Self-Treatment

Over-the-counter hemorrhoid creams, sitz baths, and fiber supplements are readily available.

If symptoms seem to ease, many assume they've treated the problem correctly—masking signs of a different underlying cause.

Lack of Awareness

Few people know about other common causes of rectal pain, such as anal fissures, abscesses, proctitis, or rectal cancer.

A 2014 guideline by the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons highlights that proper diagnosis often requires a doctor's exam—something patients may skip.

Other Common Causes of Rectal Pain

While hemorrhoids are common, here are several other reasons you might feel rectal or anal pain:

Anal FissuresSmall tears in the lining of the anus that cause sharp pain during and after bowel movements. Often linked to constipation or passing hard stools.

Perianal Abscesses & FistulasInfections around the anal canal can form painful, swollen lumps (abscesses). If they tunnel to the skin surface, they create a fistula, leading to ongoing discomfort and discharge.

ProctitisInflammation of the rectal lining from infections, inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn's), or radiation therapy. Symptoms include pain, urgency, and mucus or blood in stools.

Rectal ProlapseWhen part of the rectal wall slides out through the anus, causing pain, a feeling of fullness, or visible tissue protruding.

Pelvic Floor Muscle SpasmsStrained or tight muscles around the pelvis can refer pain to the rectal area, often felt as aching or pressure.

Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)Gonorrhea, chlamydia, herpes, or syphilis can inflame the rectal lining, leading to pain, itching, or discharge.

Rectal Ulcers & IBDChronic inflammatory conditions can cause ulcerations that burn or ache, sometimes bleeding with bowel movements.

Rectal CancerThough less common, pain, bleeding, or a change in bowel habits that doesn't resolve warrants evaluation for tumors or polyps.

Rectal Varices (in Liver Disease)Patients with advanced liver disease can develop varicose veins in the rectum similar to hemorrhoids but with a risk of heavier bleeding.

Why Misdiagnosis Matters

Delayed TreatmentAssuming hemorrhoids may postpone diagnosis of infections, abscesses, or cancer—conditions that worsen without proper care.

ComplicationsAn untreated anal abscess can spread deeper into pelvic tissues. An inflamed fissure can become chronic or lead to an infection.

Emotional StressPersistent, unexplained pain affects daily activities, sleep, and mental health.

How to Tell If It's More Than Hemorrhoids

Watch for "red-flag" signs that go beyond typical hemorrhoid symptoms:

Severe, worsening pain that doesn't improve with home remedies

Fever, chills, or general feelings of illness (possible infection)

Constant bleeding that soaks more than a couple of pads a day

A lump that's hard, tender, or increasing in size

Discharge of pus or foul odor (abscess)

Changes in bowel habits lasting more than two weeks

Weight loss, fatigue, or anemia (could indicate inflammation or cancer)

If you experience any of these, don't wait—speak to your doctor.

Steps to Take at Home

Before rushing to the ER, you can try gentle measures that help many mild cases:

Increase dietary fiber (fruits, vegetables, whole grains)

Stay well-hydrated—aim for at least 8 glasses of water daily

Take warm sitz baths (10–15 minutes, several times a day)

Avoid straining on the toilet; consider a footstool to elevate your knees

Use over-the-counter topical ointments or suppositories per package directions

When to Seek Professional Care

If home relief fails within a week

Any severe bleeding, pain, or signs of infection

New symptoms in people over 50 or those with a family history of colorectal cancer

Concern about sexual transmission or safety after intercourse

History of liver disease with possible portal hypertension

Since rectal pain often accompanies digestive disorders, checking whether Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) might be contributing to your symptoms can provide valuable insight before your doctor's appointment.

Talking with Your Doctor

Be honest and clear about your symptoms, duration, and any home treatments tried.

Mention personal or family history of bowel disease, liver disease, or cancer.

Ask about physical exams (anoscopy or proctoscopy), imaging (ultrasound or MRI), or lab tests needed for accurate diagnosis.

Discuss both short-term pain relief and long-term management strategies.

Don't Ignore Rectal Pain

Rectal pain shouldn't be a taboo topic. While hemorrhoids are a frequent and often benign cause, other conditions range from bothersome to life-threatening. Misreading discomfort as "just hemorrhoids" can lead to unnecessary delays in diagnosing infections, inflammatory diseases, or even cancer. If pain persists, worsens, or comes with any worrying signs, always speak to a doctor. Early evaluation leads to better outcomes and peace of mind.
