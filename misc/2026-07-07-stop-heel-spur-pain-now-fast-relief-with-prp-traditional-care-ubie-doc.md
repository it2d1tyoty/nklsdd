Explanation

Heel spurs—bony protrusions on the underside of the heel bone—are a common source of foot pain. While some heel spurs are painless, many trigger inflammation in the plantar fascia, causing sharp heel pain with each step. Traditional treatments often focus on reducing inflammation and relieving stress on the foot. More recently, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has emerged as a promising adjunct therapy. This article explores how combining PRP for heel spurs with established treatments may speed recovery and improve outcomes.

Understanding Heel Spurs and Plantar Fasciitis

A heel spur is a calcium deposit that can develop over months at the front of the heel bone.

Many people with heel spurs also have plantar fasciitis—inflammation of the thick band of tissue (plantar fascia) connecting the heel to the toes.

Traditional Care for Heel Spurs

Conservative treatments remain the first line of defense. Typical approaches include:

Rest and activity modification: Reduce high-impact activities that aggravate the heel.

Ice therapy: Apply ice packs for 10–15 minutes, several times daily, to reduce inflammation.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs): Short-term use of ibuprofen or naproxen to manage pain and swelling.

Stretching exercises: Calf stretches, towel stretches, and plantar fascia stretches to improve flexibility.

Orthotic devices: Heel cups, arch supports, or custom orthotics help redistribute pressure.

Physical therapy: Manual therapy, ultrasound, or laser therapy to promote healing.

Night splints: Keep the foot dorsiflexed overnight to lengthen the plantar fascia.

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT): Sound-wave therapy to stimulate tissue repair.

For most patients, symptoms improve within 6–12 months of consistent conservative care. However, up to 10% of people continue to experience significant pain despite these measures.

What Is PRP Therapy?

PRP stands for Platelet-Rich Plasma, a concentration of platelets and growth factors derived from your own blood. Key points:

Blood draw: A small sample of your blood is taken.

Centrifugation: The sample is spun to separate red blood cells from plasma.

Concentration: The plasma layer, rich in platelets (2–6 times baseline), is isolated.

Injection: Platelet-rich plasma is injected into and around the site of injury or inflammation.

PRP harnesses the body's natural healing mechanisms. Platelets release growth factors—such as PDGF, TGF-beta, and VEGF—that:

Stimulate cell proliferation

Enhance collagen production

Evidence for PRP in Heel Spurs

Research on PRP for heel spurs and plantar fasciitis suggests:

Significant pain reduction compared to baseline, often lasting 6–12 months or longer.

Improved functional scores (e.g., ability to walk or exercise).

Comparable or superior outcomes to corticosteroid injections, with a potentially lower risk of tissue atrophy.

Faster return to activity when combined with physical therapy.

Sample study findings:

A randomized trial reported 70–80% of patients achieving "excellent" or "good" outcomes at 6-month follow-up.

Another study found PRP reduced pain scores by 50–70% at 3-month follow-up.

Why Combine PRP with Traditional Care?

Integrating PRP for heel spurs into your existing treatment plan can have synergistic benefits:

Enhanced healing: Growth factors accelerate tissue repair alongside stretching and physical therapy.

Reduced reliance on NSAIDs: Potentially fewer medications and their side effects.

Longer-lasting relief: PRP may extend the benefits of orthotics and night splints.

Holistic approach: Addresses both the mechanical (stress reduction) and biological (tissue regeneration) aspects of heel pain.

What to Expect During a PRP Session

Consultation: A podiatrist or sports medicine physician evaluates your history, symptoms, and previous treatments.

Blood draw and processing: Takes about 15–20 minutes.

Local anesthetic may be applied.

Under ultrasound guidance, PRP is injected into the plantar fascia and around the heel spur.

The procedure typically lasts 10–15 minutes.

Rest the foot for 24–48 hours.

Ice the area to manage mild post-injection discomfort.

Resume stretching and low-impact exercises as recommended.

Potential Risks and Considerations

PRP is generally safe, since it uses your own blood. However, be aware of:

Mild pain or swelling at the injection site for a few days.

Infection risk—rare but possible with any injection.

Variable response—not all patients achieve significant improvement.

Cost and insurance—PRP may not be covered by all insurance plans.

Discuss these factors with your physician to determine if PRP is appropriate for you.

Aftercare and Rehabilitation

To maximize PRP benefits:

Continue your stretching routine daily.

Wear supportive shoes with good heel cushioning.

Follow physical therapy protocols—strengthening exercises for foot intrinsic muscles and calf muscles.

Use orthotics or night splints as prescribed.

Gradually reintroduce high-impact activities under professional guidance.

Who Is a Good Candidate?

PRP for heel spurs may be considered if:

You have chronic heel pain (>6 months) despite conservative care.

You wish to avoid or reduce corticosteroid injections.

Schedule follow-up visits at:

Adjust your treatment plan—additional PRP sessions, alternative therapies, or advanced imaging—if needed.

When to Seek Professional Advice

If you experience any of the following, contact your healthcare provider immediately:

Severe worsening of pain or swelling

Signs of infection: redness, warmth, fever, or drainage at the injection site

New numbness or tingling in the foot
