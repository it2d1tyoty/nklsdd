You may get anxious wondering if forgetful moments mean you’re moving into full-blown dementia, says Dr. Rush, who also sees loss of confidence and social withdrawal in her patients with MCI.

People with MCI can also experience apathy, which means they have less motivation to do things, says Joseph “Audie” Black, PhD, a clinical neuropsychologist in private practice in Boise, Idaho. But alongside medical treatment, steps you take to recharge can also lower the risk that MCI progresses to dementia, says Dr. Black.

This doesn’t mean being around just anyone, says Black. “I always say life is too short to be around jerks. It's being around people who love and care about you and make you feel positively connected.”

You may feel tempted to skip social activities, worried you might have a verbal slip-up or forget someone’s name, says Black. But staying social is vital when you have MCI, he says.

You can garden, walk in a park, or stroll down the street, says Rush. Drink your morning coffee on your front step or back patio, just to get outside and start your day with a fresh reset, she says.

Take walks outside with a friend or loved one to add the benefits of social interaction to exercise, says Black.

3. From Stretches to Dancing: Find a Way to Move More

If outside walks don’t work for you, you can still get your body moving and heart pumping. Start with stretches, says Rush. If you notice you’re sitting for a long time, gentle movements can be very helpful, she says.

Rush also suggests trying small, silly things that get you moving. “Maybe you have the music on as you're doing dishes and you just dance in your kitchen,” says Rush. “[Try] small things that allow your body to move in a free or joyful way.”

Hobbies activate your brain, and completing projects gives you a rush of dopamine, a neurotransmitter involved in learning and motivation, says Black.

You can increase the brain-boosting effects of hobbies by choosing something you’ve never done before, says Rush. Your brain does better at preserving cognitive function when you do new things, she says.

“I tell people, absolutely play games, but what I recommend is don't just play the same one every time,” says Black. Vary the games that you're playing, so you're challenging your brain in unique ways.”

5. Eat a Brain-Healthy Diet on a Schedule

“For some of my patients, they find that they are less hungry and they may not eat as often as they used to,” says Rush. With MCI and normal aging, you can lose some hunger cues, even though your body needs food, says Rush.

Food choices matter, too. Try to focus on whole, plant-based foods alongside healthy proteins like salmon, says Black. Avoid added sugars, alcohol, and highly processed foods, he says. “If you're doing that, you're making massive strides in the right direction.”

Rush suggests these volunteer possibilities.
